## 1. Executive Summary

Global Relief ELPIS is a decentralized humanitarian and no-food-waste network that combines local surplus food redistribution with global disaster relief and financial protection. At its core, the project recognizes a painful contradiction: enormous amounts of edible food are discarded daily, while millions of people face hunger, and when disasters strike, many families are left without fast and reliable support.

The project uses Cardano blockchain technology, AI, IoT and a dual-token economy to connect people who have surplus food with those who need it in their neighborhoods, while also enabling donors, NGOs and other actors to provide immediate, stable, traceable support in times of crisis. The volatile **FAI** token incentivizes food sharing, community participation and ecosystem contributions, while the **ELPIS** stablecoin, pegged 1:1 to USD, serves as a reliable humanitarian currency for disaster payouts, beneficiary support and NGO operations.

The system is built with privacy and safety at its foundation. Personally identifiable information is kept off-chain and protected through privacy-preserving mechanisms, including potential use of Midnight. Smart contracts written in Haskell Plutus enforce the rules of food listings, transactions, rewards and disaster relief distributions. Hydra Heads enable near-instant local settlements, and IoT devices plus drone integrations allow secure and verifiable transport of food and supplies, even in high-crime or physically inaccessible areas. Together, these components form a global, programmable safety net that is both technically robust and deeply human in its purpose.


## 2. Background and Problem Statement

### 2.1 The Global Food Waste Crisis

Globally, a staggering amount of edible food is wasted every single day. In many homes, families cook more than they can finish, events and gatherings generate leftovers, and supermarkets discard near-expiry items. Much of this food could still safely nourish someone if quickly re-routed. Without a convenient marketplace or coordination mechanism, however, this edible surplus is often simply thrown into rubbish bins and landfills, contributing to environmental damage and wasted resources.

### 2.2 Hunger, Poverty and Local Food Inaccessibility

At the same time, many individuals and families struggle to secure reliable, affordable meals. They may be working poor, students, migrants, unemployed people or vulnerable elderly citizens. They are often priced out of restaurant meals and commercial food delivery, and they may not always have time, facilities or skills to cook from raw ingredients every day. Traditional food outlets focus on standardized menus and fast food chains, leaving out the richer variety of cultural, home-cooked staples that might be healthier and more satisfying.

### 2.3 Disasters, Shocks and the Relief Gap

Superimposed on this everyday scenario are acute shocks: floods, fires, war, droughts, earthquakes, pandemics, industrial accidents and collisions on roads. These events can suddenly destroy homes, break supply chains and cut people off from their sources of food and income. Disaster victims often wait days or weeks for aid, and many never receive sufficient support. Relief money can become trapped in bureaucratic processes, difficult to trace, or diverted through corruption or mismanagement.

### 2.4 Limitations of Traditional Systems

Traditional food delivery platforms focus mainly on restaurants and commercially prepared foods. Food donation efforts depend on manual coordination, slow paperwork and limited traceability. Disaster relief relies heavily on centralized organizations that may not always be transparent or efficient, and beneficiaries often lack any financial instrument that is both stable and accessible during crisis. There is no unified infrastructure that simultaneously tackles food waste, everyday food access and rapid disaster relief with transparency, automation and privacy.


## 3. Vision and Mission

### 3.1 Vision: No Food Waste, No Family Abandoned

The vision of Global Relief ELPIS is a world in which no edible food is wasted and no family is left alone when disaster strikes. In this world, surplus food flows naturally from those who have “too much” to those who have “too little,” and digital infrastructures ensure that any person affected by shock—whether a flood, war, fire or economic collapse—can access stable, transparent support quickly and with dignity.

### 3.2 Mission: A Global Relief and No-Food-Waste Network

The mission is to develop and maintain a decentralized network that:

- Connects households, community cooks and donors with local buyers and beneficiaries.  
- Uses blockchain, AI, IoT and drones to coordinate safe, fast, verifiable exchanges of food and funds.  
- Incentivizes positive behavior with FAI, a token tied to nourishment and contribution.  
- Delivers stable, predictable disaster assistance using the ELPIS stablecoin.  
- Preserves privacy while keeping flows of value auditable and accountable.  
- Empowers communities, NGOs, churches and governments to collaborate on a shared humanitarian infrastructure.


## 4. Concept and Business Model

### 4.1 No-Food-Waste Marketplace

At the everyday level, Global Relief ELPIS operates a neighborhood-based marketplace where individuals and community cooks can list surplus or freshly prepared meals. A seller who realizes there is more food than their household can consume can use a mobile app or web interface to take photos, describe the dish, indicate ingredients and set an expiry time. The platform helps them price the meal in a way that reflects its freshness, cultural value and local conditions, and the listing becomes visible to nearby users on a GPS-based map.

Buyers open the same application and see a rich selection of home-cooked and community food options around them: soups, stews, breads, rice dishes, vegetables and other staples, often at significantly lower prices than restaurant meals. They can filter by price, diet, culture, distance, hygiene rating and user reviews. Some listings may be free, donated by generous households or by people who simply do not want to commit food to the bin. All of this activity is recorded through smart contracts that track orders, payments and ratings while respecting privacy.

### 4.2 Global Relief and Disaster Safety Net

Beyond everyday food sharing, the system includes a dedicated relief layer that functions like a decentralized, community-driven form of disaster insurance or safety net. Donors, including individuals, organizations, corporations and cross-chain crypto communities, can allocate funds in FAI, ELPIS, ADA or other supported assets into on-chain disaster pools targeted at specific regions or event types. When a flood, war, fire or other calamity strikes, these pools can quickly disburse ELPIS into the wallets of affected individuals or NGOs operating in the field.

Because ELPIS is pegged to the US dollar, beneficiaries receive predictable value that is not subject to the extreme price volatility of most cryptocurrencies. Smart contracts, IoT devices, AI-driven risk assessments and humanitarian partners work together to determine how much support is released, to whom and under what conditions. This transforms relief from a slow, opaque process into a transparent, programmable and verifiable digital flow of stable value.

### 4.3 Linking Everyday Surplus to Extraordinary Crises

The same infrastructure that routes surplus food within neighborhoods can be repurposed to support those affected by disasters. A community cook who regularly sells or donates food through the platform may, during a disaster, become a paid provider of meals to displaced families, with ELPIS payments flowing from disaster pools. Local couriers who normally deliver neighborhood meals might pivot to delivering emergency supplies via bikes or drones. The line between “normal operations” and “disaster response” becomes smoother, as the same social graph, reputation data and logistics networks are used for both.


## 5. Stakeholders and Ecosystem

### 5.1 Households, Community Cooks and Local Food Producers

At the grassroots, the platform empowers ordinary households, local cooks and informal food producers to monetize or donate surplus food. A person cooking a family meal can now capture value from leftovers rather than discarding them, while a talented community cook can build a reputation and customer base without needing to open a physical restaurant.

### 5.2 Buyers, Beneficiaries and Disaster Victims

Buyers include students, low-income workers, busy professionals and anyone who wants affordable, culturally familiar food. Beneficiaries include individuals who may not always be able to pay, and who can discover free or subsidized listings. Disaster victims receive ELPIS transfers and can either purchase food on the platform or use funds for essential items from participating merchants and NGOs.

### 5.3 NGOs, NPOs, Churches and Community Organizations

Nonprofit organizations, churches and community groups use the system to coordinate food distribution, manage relief programs and partner with local cooks and couriers. They can tag certain listings as part of their programs, receive donations of FAI or ELPIS, and rely on transparent, auditable flows of funds and goods.

### 5.4 Developers, Educators, Artists and Sports Partners

Developers build integrations, analytics and tools on top of the open infrastructure. Educators create training programs on food safety, nutrition and financial literacy. Artists and sports organizations participate in campaigns that raise awareness, encourage participation and attract funding, supported by dedicated FAI allocations.

### 5.5 Cross-Chain Crypto Communities and Donors

Holders of ADA, NIGHT, BTC, ETH, SOL, DOGE, XRP, BNB, TRON and other assets participate through bridges, airdrops and disaster pools. They gain a direct, transparent mechanism to support real-world humanitarian outcomes while receiving FAI incentives and reputational benefits.


## 6. System Architecture

### 6.1 Application Layer

The application layer consists of mobile apps and web interfaces that expose core functionality: listing surplus food, browsing nearby meals, requesting help, donating funds, viewing relief campaigns, managing wallets and seeing personal impact statistics. The interfaces are designed to be intuitive even for users with low technical literacy or working under stress.

### 6.2 Off-Chain Logic (Helios and Lucid)

Off-chain components use Helios and Lucid to construct and submit transactions to the Cardano network, while managing session state, AI interactions, notifications and integration with external services like SMS gateways and mapping APIs. These services maintain a clean separation between public on-chain records and private off-chain data such as user contact details or detailed addresses.

### 6.3 Smart Contract Layer (Plutus on Cardano)

Plutus smart contracts implement the logic for food listings, order escrows, dispute handling, relief pool management, FAI distribution rules, ELPIS minting and burning, and governance. Each critical flow is encoded as a state machine, with clearly specified allowed transitions, ensuring predictability and auditability. Formal methods, such as property-based testing and model checking, are used to ensure that invariants hold—for instance, that funds cannot be taken from a relief pool without meeting governance-approved conditions.

### 6.4 Hydra Heads for Local Finality

Hydra Heads allow groups of users in a local region—such as a neighborhood or a refugee camp—to interact in a high-throughput, low-latency environment. Within a Hydra Head, transactions such as small payments, refunds or reward distributions can be processed almost instantly. The closing state of the Hydra Head is periodically settled on the main Cardano chain, giving strong security while supporting real-time usage.

### 6.5 Midnight for Privacy-Preserving Identity and Cases

Midnight, or similar privacy-enhancing technologies in the Cardano ecosystem, allow sensitive information to be handled with strong confidentiality guarantees. For example, it may be necessary to prove that an individual is indeed a registered disaster victim, a verified NGO or a specific category of beneficiary without revealing their full personal details. Midnight can enable zero-knowledge proofs or shielded transactions that protect human dignity while maintaining integrity.

### 6.6 Data, Storage and Media

Off-chain databases such as PostgreSQL store structured data like user profiles, non-sensitive metadata, AI model outputs and audit logs. Decentralized storage systems like IPFS or similar solutions are used for media files, including photographs of food, scanned documents or promotional material. Hashes linking on-chain records to off-chain content ensure integrity and non-repudiation.

### 6.7 Security and Monitoring Layer

A dedicated security and monitoring layer combines log analysis, machine learning and external security tools to detect anomalies, attempted fraud, bot activity, smart contract irregularities and system abuse. This layer helps maintain trust, prevent financial loss and protect vulnerable users from exploitation.


## 7. Core Technologies

### 7.1 Cardano and the Extended UTXO Model

Cardano provides a scientifically grounded, peer-reviewed blockchain with an extended UTXO model that supports expressive, deterministic smart contracts. This model is well-suited for managing state as discrete, verifiable UTXOs, such as listings, escrows or relief pool states.

### 7.2 Haskell Plutus and Formal Methods

Plutus allows smart contracts to be written in Haskell, benefiting from a strong type system, purity and decades of functional programming research. Formal methods can be applied to reason about contract behavior and prevent common classes of bugs.

### 7.3 Helios and Lucid for UX and Off-Chain Integration

Helios provides a more ergonomic and modular way to express on-chain logic in a JS-friendly ecosystem, while Lucid enables straightforward signing, building and submitting of transactions from web and mobile environments. Together, they bridge the gap between the user experience and the core Cardano infrastructure.

### 7.4 AI Large Language Models and Analytics

AI large language models assist users in drafting and understanding content, such as writing clear food descriptions, generating safety warnings or explaining disaster relief processes. Other AI models analyze user behavior, donation patterns and environmental data to improve recommendations, safety and fairness.

### 7.5 IoT Sensors and Drone Integration

IoT sensors embedded in containers and storage environments track temperature, humidity, motion and location. Drones equipped with GPS and communication modules deliver food and supplies in areas where roads are blocked or personal safety is at risk. These devices interact with smart contracts by providing verifiable data points and signing events such as delivery confirmation.


## 8. Dual-Token Economic Model

### 8.1 Why Two Tokens: FAI and ELPIS

No single asset can adequately handle the needs of both incentives and stability. FAI is designed to float freely with market dynamics, encouraging risk-taking and rewarding early adopters. ELPIS must remain stable so that victims of disaster, NGOs and ordinary users can rely on it as a predictable unit of value.

### 8.2 FAI – Nourishment, Action and Community Growth

FAI is a Cardano-native token with a fixed total supply of 10,000,000,000 units. It represents participation, nourishment and growth. Users earn FAI by listing food, donating surplus, providing excellent service, building tools, educating others and creating cultural or sports-related initiatives that amplify the network.

### 8.3 ELPIS – Stability, Protection and Relief

ELPIS is a stablecoin pegged to the US dollar. It functions as the humanitarian currency for the ecosystem. When someone receives ELPIS during a disaster, they can be confident that its purchasing power will not fluctuate wildly. NGOs and partners can account in ELPIS as if it were dollars, but with the added benefits of programmability and transparency.

### 8.4 Interaction Between FAI and ELPIS

Users can hold FAI when they desire upside exposure and convert it into ELPIS when they want stability. Disaster pools and relief programs prefer to hold ELPIS, while reward programs and community-building initiatives rely on FAI. Liquidity pools and decentralized exchanges facilitate these conversions.

## 10. Tokenomics of ELPIS

### 10.1 Peg Mechanism and Collateralization

ELPIS is minted when users or institutions deposit eligible collateral—such as ADA, FAI, BTC, ETH or other approved assets—into a stability module governed by smart contracts. The amount of ELPIS minted is constrained by the value of the collateral and a minimum collateral ratio, typically 150% or higher. Oracles provide price feeds, and reserve accounting tracks the total outstanding ELPIS vs. collateral. When ELPIS is burned, the corresponding collateral is released back to the redeemer.

### 10.2 ELPIS in Humanitarian and Disaster Contexts

ELPIS is used for:

- Disaster victim payouts  
- NGO operational budgets  
- Stable food pricing in unstable economies  
- Grants and stipends for vulnerable populations  

Because ELPIS aims to maintain a 1:1 peg with USD, both donors and recipients can plan and budget with confidence.

### 10.3 Reserve Management and Risk Controls

Reserve management policies can adjust acceptable collateral types, minimum ratios and fees. In extreme market conditions, minting might be temporarily paused to protect the peg. Governance may direct the use of FAI or treasury reserves to shore up ELPIS reserves during stress events.


## 11. Governance: The Global Relief ELPIS DAO

### 11.1 Governance Principles

The DAO follows principles of neutrality, transparency, humanitarian focus, decentralization and evidence-based decision-making. It embraces pluralistic input while avoiding capture by any single interest group.

### 11.2 Governance Structure and Voting

FAI token holders exercise governance rights. They can vote directly or delegate voting power to representatives. Governance proposals include changes to protocol parameters, approval of new disaster pools, treasury spending, expansion into new regions and collateral additions.

### 11.3 Advisory Nodes: NGOs, Churches, Institutions

Non-token advisory roles are given to recognized humanitarian actors such as NGOs, churches, universities and community-based organizations. While they may not hold binding voting power, their recommendations and assessments are prominently visible, shaping community opinion and decisions.

### 11.4 AI-Assisted Governance and Transparency

AI tools assist with summarizing proposals, evaluating potential risks, modeling economic and humanitarian impacts and highlighting potential consequences. This does not replace human decision-making but makes governance more informed and accessible.


## 12. AI Integration in Practice

### 12.1 Food Safety and Expiry Prediction

AI models use information about dish type, ingredients, preparation time, storage conditions and local climate to suggest safe consumption windows. They prevent sellers from unintentionally listing unsafe items and warn buyers when food is nearing the end of its safety window.

### 12.2 Personalized Matching for Buyers and Beneficiaries

AI learns from users’ preferences, dietary requirements, cultural backgrounds and budgets to recommend meals and relief options that align with their needs.

### 12.3 Disaster Intelligence and Resource Routing

AI ingests data from weather services, IoT devices, satellites and on-the-ground reports to predict where and when relief will be needed. It helps direct ELPIS and food resources to the most affected communities efficiently.

### 12.4 Fraud Detection and Abuse Prevention

Behavioral patterns are monitored to detect rating manipulation, identity fraud, repeated abuse or attempts to siphon relief funds. Suspicious accounts are flagged for human review.


## 13. IoT, GPS and Drone Logistics

### 13.1 Smart Containers and Environmental Monitoring

Smart containers with embedded sensors track the integrity of food shipments or relief goods, logging temperature, humidity and travel time. Out-of-range conditions can trigger warnings or refunds.

### 13.2 GPS-Based Locality and Geofencing

GPS and geofencing ensure that food listings appear primarily within safe, local delivery ranges and that disaster relief distributions target the intended regions.

### 13.3 Drones for Secure and Hard-to-Reach Deliveries

Drones help deliver meals and supplies where crime, flooding or infrastructure damage make conventional delivery risky or impossible. They can confirm deliveries digitally, feeding data back into smart contracts.

### 13.4 Parametric Triggers for Automated Relief

IoT devices in flood-prone rivers or fire sensors in forests can trigger automated releases of ELPIS to pre-approved recipients or NGOs when certain thresholds are crossed, forming a transparent, parametric form of disaster insurance.


## 14. User Journeys

### 14.1 The Surplus Food Seller Journey

A seller cooks a meal, realizes there will be excess and opens the app. They take a photo, briefly describe the dish, and let the AI refine the wording, add allergens and suggest an expiry time. They choose whether to sell at a low price or donate. Once listed, nearby users see the item. After a buyer purchases, funds move into escrow. On delivery or pickup, both parties confirm and funds are released, with FAI rewards distributed.

### 14.2 The Local Buyer Journey

A buyer opens the app, sees a map and list of nearby meals and filters by price, culture, dietary needs or free items. The AI suggests affordable, relevant dishes. The buyer pays in ELPIS, FAI or ADA and chooses delivery or pickup. After receiving the food, they rate the seller and gain FAI incentives for constructive participation.

### 14.3 The Disaster Victim Journey

In a crisis, a victim may have lost employment, housing or local currency access. With a basic phone and wallet, they connect to Global Relief ELPIS through an NGO or on their own. If they meet eligibility criteria (verified privately via Midnight or partner organizations), they receive ELPIS directly. They can then buy food on the platform, pay trusted local merchants or store ELPIS as a buffer until conditions improve.

### 14.4 The Donor and NGO Journey

A donor opens the platform, chooses a disaster pool or a general resilience fund and contributes ELPIS, FAI or other assets. They see transparent reports on distributions, beneficiaries and impacts. NGOs use the platform to aggregate donations, schedule distributions, pay local providers and ensure that every step is logged in an auditable but privacy-preserving way.

### 15.2 Premium Features and Analytics

Sellers may pay modest subscription fees to access premium features such as advanced analytics, promotional visibility or professional branding tools.

### 15.3 Token-Based Services and Cross-Chain Flows

The platform may earn a share of fees from bridging assets across chains, providing liquidity in FAI–ELPIS–ADA pools or offering value-added financial services such as yield products tied to disaster funds.

### 15.4 Long-Term Sustainability and Public-Good Character

Global Relief ELPIS is designed to be a public good. Revenue ensures operational continuity, security and continuous improvement but must always align with the humanitarian purpose rather than maximizing profit alone.


## 16. Social, Economic and Environmental Impact

### 16.1 Food Waste Reduction and Climate Benefits

Turning surplus food into consumable offerings reduces the volume of organic waste going to landfills, thereby cutting methane emissions and easing pressure on waste management systems.

### 16.2 Poverty Reduction and Micro-Entrepreneurship

Individuals can monetize cooking skills, children can eat more reliably and local economies can benefit from micro-entrepreneurship. FAI incentives reinforce positive behaviors around food sharing and community service.

### 16.3 Disaster Resilience and Human Dignity

By providing timely, stable assistance in a transparent but privacy-protecting way, the system helps families maintain dignity and autonomy during crisis. They are not passive recipients of uncertain charity but active participants in a resilient digital network.


## 17. Legal, Regulatory and Compliance Framework

### 17.1 Food Safety and Local Regulations

The project adheres to local food safety laws. Educational content and onboarding make it clear what standards must be met, and sellers who repeatedly violate these standards can be removed.

### 17.2 Digital Assets, KYC/AML and Consumer Protection

Where required, regulated partners handle KYC/AML processes for fiat on-ramps, large donors or institutional participants. Smart contracts are audited to prevent loss and abuse.

### 17.3 Data Protection and Privacy-by-Design

Global Relief ELPIS implements privacy-by-design principles, minimizing personal data storage, applying encryption throughout and using privacy tools like Midnight to shield sensitive information while preserving accountability.
